Design for learning, not just for looking.

Five evidence-informed multimedia principles you can use tomorrow—in lecture slides, Canvas materials, clinical case reviews, radiographs, and rotation teaching.

01 · Why design matters

What does research actually show?

Learners have limited working-memory capacity. Well-designed words and pictures can reduce needless mental work and support understanding and transfer. These principles are useful design guidelines—not guarantees, and not substitutes for instruction, practice, or feedback.

Words + meaningful graphics

Mayer (2017) reports a median effect size d = 1.67 for the multimedia principle, based on 5 experimental comparisons.

Small evidence count for this estimate; do not generalize the number as a guaranteed classroom gain. Mayer, 2017

Manage unnecessary processing

In the same review, the reported median effects were coherence d = 0.70, signaling d = 0.46, and spatial contiguity d = 0.79.

Different studies, outcomes and instructional contexts. Mayer, 2017

Give processing time

For segmenting, Mayer (2017) reports a median effect size d = 0.70. Learner-paced pieces can make complex instruction easier to process.

A 2025 meta-analysis found that effects vary by multimedia type, learning outcome and design principle; some layout-design effects were smaller than common summaries imply. Recent meta-analysis

Research-to-practice caution: These numbers are standardized effects from multimedia-learning studies, not measured improvements in UF veterinary courses. Apply the principles, then check whether students can explain, interpret, and transfer the learning to cases.
02 · Explore

Five principles, five design moves

Select “Before” and “After” in each mini-example. Each pair shows the same learning target and changes only how the material is presented.

1

Coherence: remove what does not help

Design move: Keep only what directly supports the outcome. Remove decorative tangents, unnecessary pictures, sound effects and unrelated anecdotes.

Learning target: recognize an urgent GDV presentation

Apply it: A radiology slide should center on the image and interpretation task, not decorative clip art or unrelated case history.

2

Signaling: guide attention

Design move: Use a restrained arrow, label, highlight, or numbered cue to show what students should inspect first.

Learning target: identify the priority GDV cues

Apply it: Annotate the key region of an image, but do not rely on color alone—use labels or shapes too.

3

Spatial contiguity: put words near the visual

Design move: Put short, precise labels directly next to the corresponding anatomical structure or clinical finding.

Learning target: name parts of the canine ear canal

Apply it: Place a radiograph label near the anatomy, while providing an accessible text description of the image.

4

Segmenting: give learners control of the pace

Design move: Divide a demonstration or explanation into meaningful steps with a pause, question, or next-step action—not just three boxes on the same slide.

Learning target: organize an otitis externa workup

Apply it: In a clinical skills video, pause after each maneuver and ask the learner to predict what comes next.

5

Multimedia: pair words with explanatory visuals

Design move: Replace a text-only mechanism with concise wording and a relevant, accurate diagram. A decorative dog picture alone is not the same as an explanatory visual.

Learning target: explain evaporative cooling in panting

Apply it: Pair a few words with an accurate flow diagram showing the relevant mechanism. Label a conceptual schematic as such.

Keep the lesson accurate: The clinical examples are simplified educational illustrations—not stand-alone protocols. For diagnosis, treatment and clinical decisions, use current veterinary references and faculty review.

04 · Make thinking visible

Three active-learning ideas for lectures and rotations

A major meta-analysis across 225 undergraduate STEM studies found better exam performance with active learning than with traditional lectures (Freeman et al., 2014). A peer-reviewed review specifically addresses the use of structured questioning and feedback in veterinary clinical teaching (Carr et al., 2021). These sources address different populations and teaching settings; they do not establish the same effect size in veterinary courses.

Idea 1 · 3–5 minutes

Predict → Pair → Explain

Lecture: Show an uncluttered diagnostic image. Ask students to silently predict a finding, compare reasoning with a partner, then explain the evidence.

Rotation: Before revealing a test result, ask the learner what they expect and why; debrief after the result.

Design link: coherence + signaling. Promotes explanation and peer discussion; evidence base: Freeman et al.

Idea 2 · 5–7 minutes

Pause–predict–reveal

Lecture: Break a procedure video into steps. After each step, ask “What should happen next?” before continuing.

Rotation: During an appropriate, supervised teaching pause, ask the trainee to plan the next diagnostic step and give the rationale.

Design link: segmenting + signaling. Clinical education use is a proposed adaptation; the supporting theory is Mayer and structured questioning.

Idea 3 · 2–4 minutes

One-Minute Preceptor

Lecture: Give a brief case and ask for a committed diagnosis or next step with supporting evidence.

Rotation: Ask for a commitment; probe evidence; teach a general rule; reinforce what was right; correct errors with specific feedback.

Design link: one focused visual and nearby labels can support the case. Evidence and veterinary application: Neher et al.; Farrell et al.; Carr et al..

Clinical safety: Use de-identified or simulated cases in shared materials; clinical decisions remain under qualified supervision. Pause for teaching only when patient care permits.

06 · Nonnegotiable

Accessible content is effective content

An engaging diagram is not an accessible learning resource unless everyone can obtain the same essential information. Accessibility is not an optional finishing step; plan it from the beginning. UF guidance references WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the applicable minimum for covered digital content.

UF tools: Ally is integrated with UF Canvas and provides automated checks and remediation suggestions. Automated reports cannot validate all pedagogical meaning, clinical accuracy, alt text quality, or full WCAG conformance. Use manual and assistive-technology checks as well. 07 · Put it to work

Your five-minute material audit

  1. Outcome (30 seconds): What should students explain, interpret, or do after this material?
  2. Remove (60 seconds): Delete a distraction (coherence).
  3. Guide (60 seconds): Add one meaningful cue and align labels beside structures (signaling + spatial contiguity).
  4. Sequence (60 seconds): If the content is complex, create a learner-controlled pause (segmenting).
  5. Represent (30 seconds): Replace a text-only mechanism with an accurate explanatory visual (multimedia).
  6. Verify and test (60 seconds+): Confirm clinical accuracy, accessibility, and whether students can apply the idea. A thorough accessibility check may take longer.

Exit ticket for faculty

Choose one slide or case for tomorrow. Make one evidence-informed change and ask students a short application question. Capture where they struggle and revise from actual learner responses.
